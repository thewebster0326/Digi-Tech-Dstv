You open the signal screen on your DSTV decoder and see something that looks contradictory: signal strength is at or near 100%, but signal quality sits at 0. The picture is gone, the decoder says "no signal", and it seems impossible, because surely a strong signal is a good signal?

It's a common fault, and the two numbers are measuring different things. Understanding the difference makes it much easier to work out what has gone wrong.

## Signal Strength vs Signal Quality

- **Signal strength** tells you how much satellite signal energy is reaching the decoder. A high number means the dish and cable are delivering plenty of it.
- **Signal quality** tells you whether the decoder can actually decode what it's receiving into a usable picture. A quality of 0 means it can't make sense of the signal.

Think of it as hearing a loud radio station that's just static. There's plenty of noise coming in, but none of it is the broadcast you want. That's why strength can look perfect while quality is zero.

## Common Causes

**1. The dish has drifted off the satellite.** A dish that has moved slightly can end up picking up energy from a neighbouring satellite or from a poorly aimed beam. The strength reading looks healthy, but the signal isn't the DSTV one, so quality stays at zero. Strong wind, a knock, or loose mounting bolts are the usual reasons dishes move. This is the most common cause we see.

**2. The LNB has failed or is turned the wrong way.** The LNB is the small arm that sits in front of the dish. It can fail with age, water damage or heat. If it has been bumped or rotated, it can also receive signal poorly even though the dish is pointing in roughly the right direction.

**3. A cable or connector fault.** Water in the coax cable, a corroded connector, or a cable that has been pinched or chewed can let some signal through while corrupting the rest. Coastal homes see this more often because of salt air and constant damp.

**4. Interference or an obstruction.** Newly grown trees, a new building, or equipment close to the dish can degrade the signal without blocking it completely.

## What You Can Safely Check Yourself

Please don't climb onto a roof or adjust the dish by hand. Tiny movements matter enormously, and a fall is a far worse problem than a missing channel. From the ground, you can:

1. **Restart the decoder.** Unplug it for 30 seconds, plug it back in, and check the signal screen again. Menu names for the signal screen differ between decoder models, so check DSTV's help pages for yours.
2. **Check the cable at the decoder.** Make sure the satellite cable is screwed on firmly at the back of the decoder, and that you haven't plugged it into the wrong input.
3. **Look at the cable run.** Check for a cable that has been pulled tight, pinched in a window or door, or visibly damaged.
4. **Look at the dish from below.** Check whether it looks bent, tilted, or different from how it normally sits, and whether a branch or new obstruction is now in the way.
5. **Think about what changed.** A recent storm, a very windy few days, or someone working near the dish all point towards alignment.

If the readings still look the same, one of the problems above is almost certainly sitting out of reach, and it needs proper equipment to pin down.

## Why You Shouldn't Just Re-Aim the Dish Yourself

Because a dish can pick up a neighbouring satellite and give you a strong but unusable reading, the strength number alone can mislead you while you adjust it. Professional signal meters measure quality as well as strength, and the dish also has to be set at the right angle and skew. Guessing usually makes things worse.

## What a Technician Will Do

When we attend a "full strength, zero quality" call, we typically:

- check the dish alignment with a signal meter and re-aim it if it has drifted
- test the LNB and replace it if it's faulty
- check the cable and connectors, and replace damaged sections
- re-check the decoder's signal screen to confirm the quality reading is back up before we leave
